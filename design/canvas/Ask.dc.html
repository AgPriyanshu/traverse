Who is Mr Collins, and why does everyone find him so trying?
answered from the character graph, then the passages behind it

Mr Collins is a clergyman and the Bennets’ cousin. Under the entail on Longbourn he stands to inherit the estate that Mr Bennet’s five daughters cannot p. 62, which is why he arrives in chapter 13 openly intending to choose a wife among them p. 64.

He proposes to Elizabeth in chapter 19 and is refused p. 104. Three days later he proposes to Charlotte Lucas, who accepts him p. 121 — a decision the book treats as prudence rather than affection p. 123.

As for why he grates: the book shows it rather than says it. His deference to his patroness, Lady Catherine de Bourgh, is quoted at length and entirely without irony on his part p. 66, and his letter after Lydia’s elopement offers condolence and reproach in the same breath p. 296. No passage calls him tiresome outright — that reading comes from what he is given to say.

What happens to Elizabeth’s brother?
checked the roster and the household — no match

Elizabeth has no brother. The Bennets have five daughters and no son — which is the entire reason Longbourn is entailed away to Mr Collins p. 62.

If you have someone else in mind: Mr Gardiner is her uncle p. 129, and Mr Bingley becomes her brother-in-law when he marries Jane in chapter 55 p. 350.

Nothing was invented to fill the gap. When the book does not say, neither does Traverse.
